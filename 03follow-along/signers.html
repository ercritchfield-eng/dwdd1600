Who Were the Signers

Famous Signers

Of the 56 delegates who signed the Declaration of Independence in 1776, several stand out for their leadership, subsequent presidencies, and outsized historical impact.

Thomas Jefferson (Virginia)
The primary author of the Declaration. He served on the Committee of Five assigned to draft it and later became the 3rd President of the United States.
Benjamin Franklin (Pennsylvania)
At 70, he was the oldest signer. Already an internationally renowned scientist, printer, and diplomat, he helped edit Jefferson's draft and later secured French military and financial support for the war.
John Adams (Massachusetts)
The fiercest champion for independence on the Continental Congress floor, leading the debates that pushed delegates toward separation. He became the 1st Vice President and 2nd President of the United States.
John Hancock (Massachusetts)
President of the Continental Congress and the first delegate to sign. His signature is famously large and centered, cementing "John Hancock" as an American idiom for an autograph.
Samuel Adams (Massachusetts)
John Adams's second cousin and one of Boston's primary revolutionary agitators. He founded the Sons of Liberty and organized protests against British taxation policies.
Benjamin Rush (Pennsylvania)
A prominent physician, educator, and social reformer who later served as Treasurer of the U.S. Mint and was instrumental in reconciling the bitter political feud between John Adams and Thomas Jefferson later in life.
Richard Henry Lee (Virginia)
Introduced the formal "Lee Resolution" on June 7, 1776, which officially proposed that the United Colonies "are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States."
Charles Carroll of Carrollton (Maryland)
The only Roman Catholic signer and the wealthiest man in the colonies at the time. He was also the last surviving signer, living until 1832.

Each of these individuals played a significant role in the founding of the United States and is remembered for thier courage in signing the Declaration, an act cinsidered treasonous by the British Crown
